Campus Visit Slots: Plan Capacity Before Admission Season Gets Busy

Set visit slot limits, arrival instructions and rescheduling rules that reception and counsellors can manage.

Introduction

A campus visit uses several kinds of capacity: reception space, counsellor time and access to classrooms or facilities. A booking calendar that ignores these dependencies can look available while the school is already overloaded. Start with the experience the school can deliver well, then decide how many families fit each slot.

Make the slot more than a date

Define the visit duration, arrival window, meeting point, expected group size and staff owner. Keep additional time between visits where staff must write notes or move between buildings. Identify periods that cannot accept visitors because examinations or other activities take priority.

Separate requested bookings from confirmed slots. The confirmation should explain how to reschedule and whom to contact on arrival. If two staff members can change the calendar, use one shared capacity record so they cannot independently fill the same remaining place.

Example: a Saturday morning queue

Suppose two counsellors can each handle one family at a time. Four bookings at 10:00 do not become workable simply because each visit is short. Offer staggered arrivals or a clearly described group orientation followed by individual meetings. Record that arrangement in the confirmation so parents do not expect a private appointment immediately.

Review the actual experience

Track late arrivals, no-shows, reschedules and visits that started late. Do not treat a rescheduled visit as an additional new family. Compare planned and actual duration before increasing capacity. A smaller number of well-run appointments is easier to learn from than a crowded day with incomplete notes and forgotten follow-ups.

Describe the visit the school is actually offering

A visit can be a private appointment, a group orientation, a campus tour or a combination of activities. Define that format before deciding how many bookings to accept. Families should know whether they will meet a counsellor individually or join a scheduled presentation with other visitors.

Identify the beginning and end of the visit from the family's point of view. Arrival checks, waiting, movement between buildings and a final conversation all consume time even when the formal presentation is short. A calendar based only on presentation time can leave reception managing an unexpected queue.

Keep the description accurate when the format changes. If the school combines appointments into a group event, update the confirmation rather than allowing families to arrive expecting private meetings. Capacity planning works best when the offered experience, staff plan and parent instructions describe the same arrangement.

Identify the limiting resource for each stage

List the resources required at each point in the visit. Reception may have a limited waiting area, counsellors may handle one family at a time and a tour may depend on a staff escort. A vacant calendar slot does not establish that all these resources are available together.

Find the stage that constrains the whole experience. Adding another tour group will not solve a bottleneck at the individual counselling desk. Likewise, adding a counsellor may not help if the only available tour route conflicts with an examination activity.

Use the actual staffing and facilities agreed for that day. Do not rely on an employee being free because their teaching timetable looks empty; they may have another duty. Record the owner who confirms each resource so a booking decision is based on a coordinated plan rather than separate assumptions.

Include preparation and hand-back time

A counsellor needs time to review the next enquiry and record useful notes after a meeting. A tour guide may need to return to the starting point or prepare materials. If appointments are placed immediately back to back, those activities either disappear or push delays into later slots.

Build reasonable buffers from the school's observed experience and review them after actual visits. A buffer is not wasted capacity when it supports an accurate handover or prevents several families waiting at once. The appropriate duration depends on the visit format and the work involved.

Do not remove every gap to make the booking calendar appear full. First identify why the gap exists. If the school changes the format or provides separate support for preparation, the capacity may change, but the revised plan should be tested rather than assumed.

Keep one authoritative capacity record

When reception and counsellors can both accept bookings, they need a shared view of the remaining places. Two independent spreadsheets can each show the last slot as available and produce an overbooking without either staff member knowingly exceeding capacity.

Agree which record governs confirmation and who may alter capacity. A request received through another channel should be entered or checked against that record before being promised to the family. Avoid treating an informal message to a staff member as a confirmed appointment unless the booking process recognises it.

If the booking tool does not prevent conflicts automatically, define the manual check clearly. The person confirming the appointment should verify the current allocation and record the confirmation immediately. The school can then investigate an overbooking through a visible sequence instead of trying to reconstruct several private conversations.

Distinguish requested, held and confirmed places

A family asking about a time has not necessarily accepted it. If the school temporarily holds a place while awaiting a response, define how that hold is recorded and when it is reviewed. Do not leave an indefinite reservation that staff cannot distinguish from a confirmed visit.

The confirmation event should have a clear meaning, such as an agreed date and slot communicated through the approved channel. Record any relevant group-size information needed for the format. The purpose is to plan the visit, not to gather unrelated personal details about every attendee.

When a hold is released, follow the agreed communication process. Avoid offering the same place to another family while the first family reasonably believes it is still reserved. Clear states protect both the capacity plan and the credibility of the school's messages.

Make arrival instructions part of the booking

Provide the date, arrival window, meeting point and official contact route in the confirmation. If visitors need a particular entrance or check-in process, explain it in language a first-time family can understand. An internal room code may be familiar to staff but unhelpful at the gate.

Keep reception and the relevant arrival team aligned with those instructions. A parent should not be directed to a different location because the staff list came from an earlier version of the plan. Assign one owner to communicate changes that affect the visit journey.

Ask how the school will handle an unexpected delay or a family arriving early. The answer should be workable within the agreed resources. Do not solve every early arrival by starting the next appointment immediately if that displaces a preparation task or another confirmed family.

Plan for stated access needs through an appropriate route

Families may ask about the route, waiting arrangements or another requirement that affects their ability to attend. Provide an official way to raise those questions before the visit. Share only the information needed with the staff responsible for the arrangement.

Do not promise an arrangement until the relevant owner has confirmed it. A counsellor may be able to receive the request without having authority to change a facility or staffing plan. Record the question and response so the family receives accurate information rather than a well-intended assumption.

Include the confirmed arrangement in the operational briefing where appropriate. Keep sensitive context restricted; reception may need to know which route to use without receiving unnecessary personal details. The aim is to make the offered visit clear and workable, not to turn the booking form into a broad collection of private information.

Use a realistic Saturday planning example

Imagine a fictional school with two counsellors available for individual meetings and one staff member leading tours. Each family needs both a meeting and a tour. Four private meetings cannot all start at the same time merely because the tour guide can take a group afterwards.

The school can stagger the meetings and coordinate tour departures, or clearly offer a group orientation followed by scheduled individual conversations. Either approach needs a timetable that accounts for the actual sequence. Families should receive the format that the school has planned, not a vague invitation to arrive at ten.

After the session, compare planned and actual start times and record where queues formed. If meetings regularly run longer because parents bring complex questions, adjust the format or allocation. Do not simply assume that every counsellor should work faster. The plan needs to reflect the service the school is choosing to provide.

Handle rescheduling without inflating demand

A rescheduled visit is normally a change to an existing booking, not a new family enquiry. Preserve the relationship to the original applicant or family record so the admission report does not count repeated arrangements as additional demand.

Release the original slot through the authoritative capacity process and confirm the replacement only after checking availability. Record whether the family or school requested the change where that distinction helps review. Avoid leaving both appointments active because the new booking was created without resolving the earlier one.

If the school changes the date, communicate the revised arrangement and check whether the family accepts it. A new date written into a calendar does not establish agreement. The unresolved response should remain visible so reception does not prepare for an appointment that the family never confirmed.

Define late-arrival and no-show handling

Agree what staff should do when a family arrives after its planned start. Depending on the format, the school may be able to accommodate the visit, offer a later opening or reschedule. The actual options should protect the experience of other confirmed visitors as well as the arriving family.

Do not let every employee invent a different response at the desk. Provide a decision owner and a respectful explanation of the available arrangement. Where the visit is shortened or changed, make that clear rather than implying that the original full experience is still being delivered.

Record a no-show separately from a cancellation or a visit that was rearranged. Before drawing conclusions, check whether the school sent accurate instructions and whether a reschedule was recorded elsewhere. The category should describe the observed event, not a judgment about how interested the family is.

Protect school activities when approving visitor windows

Visit capacity must account for the normal work of the school. A classroom demonstration, specialist-room tour or examination period may have restrictions determined by the responsible staff. Confirm the route and timing before promising access to a particular activity.

Avoid treating a tour itinerary as permanent when the school calendar changes. A special event or room closure can affect a previously workable route. The visit coordinator needs a current source for those changes and a way to update the offered experience.

If part of the visit becomes unavailable, decide whether to provide an alternative explanation, change the route or reschedule through the approved process. Tell families what to expect. An accurate, modest description is preferable to advertising an activity that the staff cannot responsibly deliver on the day.

Measure capacity through experience, not booking totals alone

A fully booked calendar can still represent a poorly managed day if families wait without information and counsellors have no time to record outcomes. Review actual start delays, visit duration, cancellations and the completion of follow-up notes alongside the number of confirmed places.

Keep the measures clearly defined. A booked visit, an attended visit and a completed counselling conversation are separate events. The school should not use the largest available number as a general measure of success.

Ask staff which constraint caused the most disruption and examine representative cases before changing the limit. A longer arrival queue might reflect unclear instructions rather than insufficient counsellor capacity. A useful review identifies the process change that would improve the next session, not just a target for more bookings.

Publish the checked plan and retain its owner

Before the visit day, confirm the final allocations, staff responsibilities, route and parent instructions. Make the current version identifiable to reception and counsellors. If a late change occurs, update the same authoritative record and notify the people whose tasks are affected.

Keep a short closing review after the session. Record unfinished parent questions with owners and next actions rather than leaving them in a guide's memory. Capacity planning succeeds when the visit produces a clear next step as well as a completed appointment.

When evaluating Schoolment's available admission tools, use a fictional day with rescheduling, overlapping requests and a changed staff assignment. Ask which conflicts the configured system handles and which need a coordinator's check. The relevant result is a visit plan that people can execute, not merely a calendar that accepts another entry.
