Introduction

An ERP shortlist becomes more useful when each option is tested against your school's actual requirements. Admissions staff, teachers and accounts teams may judge the same product differently. This framework brings those perspectives together so you can assess workflows, implementation and support using a consistent set of questions.

Why this is a framework, not a ranked list

You'll find plenty of "Top 10 School ERP" roundups online — most are written by people who haven't run all ten products against a real school's data, and vendor claims change faster than any list can track. Rather than publish a ranking we can't independently verify, here's the framework we'd actually use to evaluate any vendor, including us.

Score every vendor against the same criteria

  • Data isolation — one database per school, or shared infrastructure?
  • Total cost — is migration and ongoing support included, or billed separately after signup?
  • Automation control — does your admin control what runs automatically, or is everything on by default?
  • Parent self-service — fees, attendance, and marks visible to parents directly, or only through your office?
  • Real support responsiveness — test this before committing, not after: email their support address with a real question and see how they respond.
  • Exit terms — can you export your own data if you ever leave?

Ask for a demo with your own data, not a canned one

A generic demo with sample students tells you very little about how a product handles your school's actual class structure, sections, and fee categories. Ask specifically for a demo using something close to your real setup.

Where to start

Our 10 questions to ask before buying post covers the exact questions to bring to that demo — for any vendor, including Schoolment.

Score depth where you will actually live, not breadth

Every vendor's module count looks impressive on a slide, and module counts are close to meaningless as a comparison. What matters is depth in the four or five areas your school touches daily, and the specifics below are where products genuinely differ:

  • Fees — multiple heads, instalments, concessions and scholarships, late-fee rules, financial-year receipt numbering, and where the money settles.
  • Attendance — daily versus period-wise, whether the timetable drives it, and how staff leave and substitution connect.
  • Exams — weighted grading, rank with tie handling, multiple exams per term, and whether report cards regenerate cleanly after a correction.
  • Admissions — pipeline visibility, duplicate detection, a public application form, and one-click conversion to an enrolled student.
  • Communication — the channel parents actually use, and whether announcements leave a record.

Ask what happens at the year boundary

This is the single most revealing question in an ERP demo, and almost nobody asks it. What happens to last year's data when you promote students into the new academic year?

The answer you want is that promotion creates a new enrolment for the new year while the previous year's record stays exactly as it was, filterable at any time. The answer you do not want involves an archive, an export, or a support ticket — because every board query, accreditation request and transcript you will ever produce depends on multi-year data being live rather than filed.

Test the reporting layer with a question you actually have

Vendors demo dashboards that were designed to demo well. Bring a real question instead — "show me every student in Class 8 with attendance below 75% who also has pending fees" — and watch whether it takes a filter or a support request.

The follow-up question matters as much: can we build that view ourselves and keep it? A drag-and-drop dashboard builder, a report builder and CSV exports mean the school can answer its own questions next year without going back to the vendor. If every new question is a change request, you have bought a reporting dependency.

Verify the ownership questions in writing

Six items belong in the contract or in an email you keep, not in a conversation:

  • Data isolation — own database per school, or shared infrastructure?
  • Password storage and transport security — hashing algorithm, and HTTPS everywhere.
  • Access control — per-module view and manage rights enforced on every request, custom roles, two-factor authentication.
  • Consent and retention — where consent is recorded, how long records are kept, and what deletion means.
  • Export — can you take all of your own data out, in what format, at what notice.
  • Add-ons — which modules are licensed separately, so the quoted price is the real price.

Run a two-week pilot before signing a year

Demos test the vendor's ability to demo. A short pilot on one class tests the product. Two weeks with real data covers a full attendance cycle and enough of a fee cycle to be informative:

  • Import one class's real student records — bulk spreadsheet import should make this a task, not a project.
  • Have the actual class teacher mark attendance for two weeks, on their own phone, without training wheels.
  • Raise and collect one fee for that class, end to end, including a partial payment and a receipt.
  • Link a handful of guardians to the portal and ask them, plainly, whether they found it usable.
  • Ask support a real question mid-pilot and time the reply.

Then score it against the same criteria as everyone else — including us. The ten questions to ask before buying and the vendor comparison framework cover the rest, and a demo can run on your own school's context if you want one.

Evaluate the records that cross department boundaries

An admission is not finished when an application form is saved. The resulting student record must reach the correct section, fee structure, attendance register and guardian account. Use this cross-department journey as one evaluation scenario. Include a midyear admission and an approved concession so the supplier has to show the details that create work in a real school.

Ask which identifier connects the records and what happens if a name or contact number changes. The system should not depend on spelling a student's name identically across separate imports. Where a process still requires exporting and re-uploading data, record the owner and expected frequency. Those manual joins belong in the evaluation score.

Check communication in the family's actual context

Have the demonstration use a phone-sized screen, more than one linked child and a realistic school notice. Check that the guardian can identify the academic year, find a receipt and understand an attendance entry. If the school communicates in multiple languages, test its own approved wording rather than assuming every character and layout will render correctly.

Ask how staff identify failed messages and unlinked accounts. A message marked sent is not necessarily evidence that the family understood it or even received it. The school needs a practical follow-up route for families whose contact details are incomplete or whose device access is limited. Include that operational work in rollout planning.

Make the pilot answer specific questions

  • Can the class teacher finish the normal attendance task without administrator assistance?
  • Can accounts reconcile a sample bill through charge, concession, payment and balance?
  • Can a coordinator retrieve a prior-year record without changing the current-year setup?
  • Can a guardian see the correct child and only the information intended for that account?
  • Can the school export a useful dataset with the relationships needed for later migration?

Review the evidence before expanding the rollout

At the end of the pilot, classify problems as training, configuration, data quality or missing capability. Each category needs a different response. Repeating training will not fix an unsupported grading rule, while replacing software will not correct an inconsistent opening ledger. Assign an owner and an acceptance check to every issue that blocks wider use.

Keep the final evaluation focused on your institution's results. A product that suits a large multi-campus group may create unnecessary administration for a smaller school, while a simple tool may not support a complex campus. The purpose of the framework is to expose that fit with repeatable evidence, not to declare one universal winner for every Indian school.

Evaluate the whole school workflow

Use these checks when reviewing the process with the people who will operate it. Record what was demonstrated and assign any unresolved issue to an owner before expanding the rollout.
