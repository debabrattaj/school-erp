What a learning management system actually does — content delivery, assignments, assessment and progress tracking — what it does not do, and which parts a school ERP already covers.

Introduction

Teaching across classroom and online settings calls for a clear place to share material, receive assignments and review progress. An LMS can support that organisation, but the learning activities and teacher feedback still need careful planning. This guide explains the core workflows and how they relate to the rest of a school's software.

The one-sentence answer

A learning management system is the place where teaching material lives, gets delivered to students, and gets marked. Its subject is a course; its users are teachers and students; its central question is whether a particular student has worked through a particular piece of learning.

That is a narrower job than most vendor pages suggest, and the narrowness is the point. An LMS is not trying to run your school. It is trying to run your lessons.

1. It stores and delivers course content

The core function. Material for a subject is organised into a structure — units, chapters, topics, weeks — and made available to the students enrolled in that course rather than emailed as attachments or shared through a class group.

  • Documents, slides, worksheets and reading material, versioned so students see the current one.
  • Recorded video lessons, and in some platforms live class delivery.
  • External links and third-party resources gathered into the same place as the school's own material.
  • Sequencing and release rules — a unit that opens after the previous one, or on a date.
  • SCORM or similar packaged courseware, where the school buys content rather than making it.

The value is not storage, it is that a student who missed a week knows exactly what to catch up on, and a teacher covering for a colleague can see what has been taught.

2. It handles assignments and submissions

The second core function, and often the one schools adopt an LMS for. An assignment is set against a course, students submit against it, and the submission carries a timestamp — which removes an entire category of argument.

Teachers mark inside the system, leave feedback against the work rather than in a separate message, and can return it for revision. Plagiarism checking is a common add-on. Group submissions, resubmissions and late-submission rules are standard. The practical effect is that a teacher's marking pile is a queue rather than a stack of paper, and a student's history of submitted work exists in one place.

3. It assesses, and often auto-grades

Quizzes and tests built inside the LMS are the assessment layer: multiple choice, short answer, matching, and question banks that randomise across students. Objective questions grade themselves, which makes frequent low-stakes testing practical in a way paper never did.

This is the function that overlaps most with a school ERP's exam module, and where schools most often end up paying twice. Worth being precise about the difference: an LMS quiz measures progress through the material it delivered. A school's formal examination — weighted across periodic tests, term exams and practicals, with grade bands, rank and a report card at the end — is an administrative record the school is accountable for. Those are different jobs even when both are multiple-choice questions on a screen.

4. It tracks progress through the material

The reporting an LMS does is course-shaped, not school-shaped:

  • Which students have opened, started or completed each unit.
  • Time spent on material, where the platform measures it.
  • Scores across quizzes within a course, and trends within that course.
  • Which topics the cohort found hardest, by question-level performance.
  • Completion against a required path, for anything with a certification or compliance angle.

Notice what is absent from that list: fees, attendance for the school day, transport, the report card, the student's whole file. An LMS knows a student as a learner on a course. It does not know them as a member of the school.

5. It gives teachers a place to communicate about the work

Announcements scoped to a course, discussion threads on a topic, and feedback attached to a specific submission. This is subject-matter communication between a teacher and their students, and it is genuinely different from school-to-parent communication.

Parent-facing communication — fee reminders, attendance alerts, circulars, payment confirmations — is not what an LMS is built for, and schools that try to use one for it end up with parents who never log in. That traffic belongs on the channel families already use, which in India means WhatsApp and email. See WhatsApp for Parents.

What an LMS does not do

The list is longer than most schools expect, and every item on it is something a school still has to run somewhere:

  • Admissions. No enquiry pipeline, no application form, no conversion to an enrolled student.
  • Fees and accounts. No fee structures, no billing, no online collection, no receipts, no ledger, no payroll.
  • School attendance. An LMS may record attendance in an online class; it does not run daily or period-wise attendance for the school day, and it has no timetable behind it.
  • Report cards. No weighted grading across exam types, no rank, no board-shaped report card, no multi-year academic record.
  • Campus operations. No hostel, transport, library, inventory or mess.
  • Staff administration. No teacher records, leave balances or substitution.
  • Documents. No transfer certificates, bonafide certificates, transcripts or ID cards.

Which LMS functions a school ERP already covers

Before buying an LMS, check what you already have. Schoolment is an ERP, but its academic and LMS modules cover a meaningful share of what smaller schools would otherwise buy one for:

  • Learning resources — study material published to a class and optionally one section: an uploaded document, a video or external link, or a written note. Each can be tied to a syllabus unit so a chapter's material sits with the chapter, held as Draft until you publish, and released on a future date so a teacher can prepare a term in one sitting.
  • Who has actually opened it — each resource records which students in the class have read it and which have not, which is the question a teacher actually asks.
  • Homework — set against a class and section, visible to students and parents in the portal and mobile app.
  • A homework drop-box — students (or a guardian, recorded as such) hand in text and an attachment; a phone photo of the work is the realistic submission on mobile. One submission per student, replaced rather than piled up on resubmission.
  • Grading with feedback — teachers see the whole class at once: submitted, late, and not handed in. Marks and feedback are entered inline and appear to the family in the portal once marked. Lateness is stamped at submit time, so moving a due date later cannot un-late work that was handed in late, and a submission freezes once graded so a mark can never end up attached to work swapped out afterwards.
  • Courses — self-paced, blended (lessons plus instructor-led sessions, with attendance taken), or a plain e-material shelf with no sequence at all. Scoped to a class and section, or school-wide for something like staff induction.
  • Sections and ordered lessons — a lesson can be text, a link, a video, a document, or a pointer to study material, a SCORM package, an online test, an assignment or a session.
  • Prerequisites and completion rules — at course and lesson level, with each lesson stating how it is deemed finished: opening it, handing something in, reaching a minimum score, or the learner ticking it off. Optional lessons never block what comes after.
  • Progress — a percentage per enrollment, recomputed from lesson progress rather than incremented, so one figure means one thing.
  • Course feedback collected from the learners who took it.
  • SCORM packages (opt-in) — uploaded and run inside the portal. Off by default, because hosting web content is a commitment a school should choose.
  • Discussion forums (opt-in) — per course, off by default, because student-visible discussion is a safeguarding decision rather than a default.
  • Syllabus and lesson plans — held per subject, so coverage through the term is a record rather than a teacher's private notebook.
  • Multi-curriculum planner — for schools running more than one curriculum.
  • Online tests (licensed add-on) — auto-graded assessments whose results flow into the same marks record as written exams.
  • Exam proctoring (licensed add-on) for remote assessment.

The one honest exclusion left is video file hosting: a video lesson is a link, and uploads are images and PDFs up to 5 MB, so recorded lessons live on whatever platform already hosts them. SCORM packages are the exception — those are uploaded and served. Everything else an LMS is usually bought for is here. If you are pricing a separate LMS, price this first — the honest question is no longer whether your ERP covers some of it, but whether the platform you are being sold does anything your ERP does not. How to tell whether your school actually needs one

Four questions that settle it faster than a demo:

  • Does teaching depend on digital content? If lessons are delivered in a classroom and the digital layer is homework and the occasional worksheet, an LMS is a large tool for a small job.
  • Do you deliver anything self-paced? Blended programmes, remedial tracks, holiday courses and board-prep modules are the genuine use case.
  • Will teachers maintain the content? An LMS is only as good as what is loaded into it, and that is ongoing work by the people who are already busiest.
  • Would students actually log in? Adoption decides this more than features. A platform half the class never opens is worse than a well-run homework list in the parent portal.

If you run both, decide the authority first

Plenty of schools end up with an ERP and an LMS, and the integration question is simpler than vendors make it sound. One system must be the authority for student identity and enrolment — that is the ERP, because it owns admission, class placement, transfers and withdrawals. The LMS consumes that list rather than maintaining its own.

Two independently maintained student lists diverge within a term, and reconciling them lands on the same person who was already doing the manual work you bought software to remove. Bulk spreadsheet import and CSV exports make the handover practical without a custom integration project. The full comparison is in School ERP vs LMS, and what a school ERP does covers the other half.

Walk through one lesson from publication to feedback

Begin with the teacher's intended learning activity. The teacher publishes a resource, sets an assignment and tells students what a satisfactory submission should contain. Students open the material, complete the work and submit it through the agreed route. The teacher then reviews the submission, records feedback and decides whether another attempt is needed.

An LMS should make the status of that sequence visible. A file upload alone does not tell the teacher whether a student submitted, whether feedback was released or whether a revised attempt is waiting. Ask for a demonstration of the whole lesson workflow, including a late submission and a student who needs to resubmit.

Separate opening a resource from understanding it

A progress indicator can show that a resource was opened or a lesson was marked complete. It does not by itself establish understanding. Teachers need to decide what evidence matters for the activity: a response, a discussion, a completed task or an assessment. Configure completion rules to match the teaching purpose rather than treating every click as equivalent learning.

Use progress information to identify where a teacher should investigate. A student who has not opened material may have an access problem, an unclear instruction or another difficulty. The system can surface the missing activity, but the teacher provides the context and chooses the appropriate response.

Test the student experience on the devices they use

  • Open the resource on a phone and check document readability and link behaviour.
  • Submit a realistic attachment and confirm the student can see that it was received.
  • Review how due dates, late work and resubmission instructions are displayed.
  • Check that feedback becomes visible at the intended point in the teacher's workflow.
  • Provide an agreed alternative where device or connectivity limitations prevent participation.

Decide how course results relate to official school results

If the school also uses an ERP, agree whether LMS scores remain formative feedback or contribute to the official assessment record. Define which score transfers, who approves it and how later corrections are handled. A course completion percentage and a term grade describe different things and should not be treated as interchangeable simply because both are numbers.

At the end of a course or term, preserve the material and student work according to the school's approved process. Decide what next year's class inherits and what remains part of the earlier cohort's record. A useful LMS supports a repeatable teaching workflow while keeping course content, individual submissions and official academic reporting clearly understood.

Engagement in hybrid and blended classrooms

An LMS improves engagement when it gives every lesson a predictable digital home. Students attending in person can revisit instructions and resources; students joining remotely can follow the same sequence; and an absent student can see what changed without waiting for a private message. This continuity matters more than adding animation to every screen.

Digital assignment submission creates a visible cycle: assigned, opened, submitted, reviewed and returned. Teachers can find students who have not started, respond before a deadline and return feedback without losing a notebook in transit. Interactive quizzes provide quick checks for understanding, but frequent low-stakes use is usually more useful than turning every activity into a scored competition.

Resource sharing supports different formats and pacing. A teacher can publish a short reading, diagram, linked video, SCORM activity or revision sheet in the order students need it. Completion data indicates participation, not mastery, so combine it with discussion, student work and assessment. Design for the phones and bandwidth families actually have, provide captions or transcripts where appropriate and keep essential instructions readable without a large download.

Hybrid teaching also needs belonging. Discussion spaces, instructor-led sessions and timely feedback help remote students remain part of the class rather than becoming a second audience. Establish response expectations, moderate student-visible discussion and use class-level patterns to adjust teaching. The learning management system provides continuity and evidence; the teacher creates the relationship and learning design that make engagement meaningful.

FAQ

Common questions

What does LMS stand for?

Learning Management System. It is the platform that stores and delivers course content, collects and marks assignments, runs quizzes, and tracks each student's progress through learning material. Common examples in education include Moodle, Google Classroom and Canvas. It is a teaching tool, not an administrative one — fees, admissions, attendance for the school day and report cards belong to a school ERP.

Is Google Classroom an LMS?

It covers the core of one — distributing material, setting and collecting assignments, and returning marked work — with the advantage that most schools already have the accounts. What it is lighter on is structured course sequencing, question banks with randomisation, detailed progress analytics and packaged courseware support. For many schools it is enough, and the honest question before buying anything heavier is which specific thing Classroom is failing to do.

Can an LMS replace a school ERP?

No. An LMS has no admissions pipeline, no fee billing or collection, no school-day attendance or timetable, no board-shaped report cards, no campus operations and no staff administration. Some LMS vendors have added administrative modules, and some have since withdrawn them — verify current module availability directly rather than trusting a feature page, particularly for anything as consequential as fee reconciliation.

Do we need an LMS if our ERP already has online tests and homework?

Probably not. Alongside homework and syllabus, Schoolment now publishes study material to a class with per-student read tracking, and takes homework in through a drop-box that teachers grade with feedback. Between that and auto-graded online tests, most K-12 day schools are covered. The clear case for a separate LMS is recorded lessons as a core part of teaching, packaged courseware, or sequenced self-paced tracks — where content delivery is the product rather than a supplement.

Which should a school buy first, an ERP or an LMS?

For most K-12 schools, the ERP — not because teaching matters less, but because the administrative failure modes are more expensive. Fee leakage across a term, a missed admissions season, or an attendance record you cannot produce for the board costs money and standing. A missing content platform costs convenience. The exception is a school whose teaching model depends on digital delivery, where the LMS is core infrastructure.
