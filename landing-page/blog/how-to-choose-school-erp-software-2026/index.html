Using this guide

The checklists and worked examples in this guide are recommended school procedures and evaluation questions. They are not a promise that every step is automatic. Confirm the modules enabled for your school and rehearse the workflow before relying on it.

Assigned-class restrictions now apply to core student, attendance, marks, exam and homework record access for the built-in Teacher role. A teacher login must match the staff email and a class assignment. Check each enabled module and custom role separately when reviewing access.

Start with the problem, not the feature list

Most schools don't need "the most features" — they need the specific things that are currently manual and painful to stop being manual: fee reminders, attendance registers, report card season, or admissions follow-ups. Write down the 3-4 things costing your office the most time before you look at a single product.

A working shortlist checklist

Before you book a demo with anyone, confirm these:

  • Data isolation — is your school's data in its own database, or pooled with other schools on the same product?
  • Migration and support cost — are these included, or sold separately after you're already committed?
  • Automation is opt-in — can your admin control exactly what runs automatically (fee generation, promotions, exam schedules), or does everything fire by default?
  • Parent-facing self-service — can parents check fees, attendance, and marks themselves, or does every question become a phone call to your office?
  • Exit plan — can you actually export your own data if you ever need to leave?

Demo the modules you'll actually use every week

Fee collection, attendance, and exams are used constantly — spend most of the demo there, not on rarely-used reports. Ask to see a real task end-to-end: mark attendance for a full class, generate a report card, or record a fee payment — not just a slide deck.

Match the product to the shape of your school

Schools of different shapes need genuinely different things, and a product that suits one is often wrong for another. Find yourself in this list before you shortlist:

  • Single campus, up to about 800 students. Depth in fees, attendance and exams matters more than anything else. Included support and migration matter more than configurability.
  • Single campus with hostel or heavy transport. Campus operations stop being optional — beds, routes, live tracking and mess records need to be in the same system, or they become four more spreadsheets.
  • Multi-branch trust. Ask specifically how a common fee policy, consolidated reporting and per-branch access are handled. This is where most single-campus products struggle.
  • School plus junior college under one trust. Check that multiple curricula and differing exam schemes are supported without a second system.
  • School with substantial online teaching. You likely need an LMS alongside an ERP — decide which is the authority for student identity before you buy either.

Count the total cost of the first year

Per-student pricing is the number vendors compete on and the number that predicts least. Add these up instead, and compare the totals:

  • Subscription for the year at your actual enrolment, not a starting tier.
  • Migration of existing records — included, or a separate line?
  • Onboarding and training, for enough weeks to cover a fee cycle and an exam season.
  • Any modules licensed separately as add-ons.
  • Per-transaction fees on fee collection — on school volumes this can exceed the subscription itself.
  • Staff hours during the transition, which are real even though nobody invoices for them.

Involve the people who will use it daily, before you decide

ERP decisions are usually made by a principal or trustee and lived with by an accounts clerk and forty teachers. That gap is the most reliable predictor of a failed rollout in this category.

Put the shortlist in front of three people before signing: the accountant who will do fee reconciliation every week, one class teacher who will mark attendance every day, and the office staff member who answers the phone during fee season. Ask each of them to complete one real task in each product. Their verdict on which is faster is worth more than any feature comparison, because they are the ones whose workaround will quietly undermine the system if it is slower than what they do today.

Configurability you should expect on day one

A product that requires a vendor release to rename a field will require a vendor release for everything, and that dependency shapes the next three years. What a school should be able to change itself:

  • Reorder, rename and add fields on a student record, and decide which are mandatory.
  • Build custom roles — an academic coordinator who manages exams but only views fees, an accountant who never sees counselling notes — with per-module no access, view or manage rights.
  • Set which modules are switched on, so staff only see what the school uses.
  • Apply the school's own branding to the portal and to generated documents.
  • Choose the interface language, including right-to-left layouts where the language calls for it.
  • Control every automated schedule, and see a log of what ran.

A four-week decision timeline

Long evaluations rarely produce better decisions than structured short ones. A month is enough if the weeks have jobs:

  • Week 1: write down the three or four things costing your office the most time, and turn them into a weighted scorecard. Shortlist three vendors, no more.
  • Week 2: demos, driven by your five real tasks rather than their slides. Email each support address with a genuine question and time the replies.
  • Week 3: a short pilot on one class with real data — import, two weeks of attendance, one fee raised and collected, a handful of guardians linked.
  • Week 4: score against the sheet, get the contract answers in writing, and decide.

Turn the shortlist into an acceptance exercise

Write five tasks that would make daily work easier at your school. Examples might include admitting a midyear student, collecting a partial fee, correcting attendance, publishing a report card and giving a guardian access to two children. For each task, define what a successful result looks like and who needs to approve it. Send these scenarios before the demonstration.

Ask the vendor to complete each task with realistic fictional records, then let the intended staff member repeat it. Note where the person needs help, where an administrator must intervene and where information has to be entered twice. This produces a record of operational fit that is easier to compare than the number of modules in a brochure.

Define launch conditions before signing off

A launch condition is something the school must be able to verify. For example, every active student has an approved section, sampled balances match the source ledger and teachers can open only the classes they manage. Agree which party supplies the data, which party imports it and who checks the result. Put unresolved configuration questions in a named issue list.

Avoid treating installation as the completion milestone. The software may be available while staff access, fee rules or family onboarding remain unfinished. Define the point at which the school accepts the migrated records and begins normal work. This gives both the school and supplier a shared understanding of readiness.

Ask for a written scope that staff can understand

  • List the modules, campuses and user groups included in the proposed setup.
  • Identify optional services, integrations and usage charges separately from the base service.
  • State the migration inputs, supported historical records and responsibilities for data cleaning.
  • Describe training by staff role and the route for follow-up questions after launch.
  • Confirm the available export formats and the process for obtaining school records later.

Use disagreement as a signal to investigate

The accounts team may prefer one product while teachers prefer another. Ask each group to show the task behind its preference. A disagreement about a familiar interface can be resolved through a short trial; a disagreement about missing fee controls or assessment rules may reveal a substantive gap. Keep the comparison grounded in observable work.

The final recommendation should explain why the selected system fits the school's highest-priority tasks, which limitations remain and how those limitations will be managed. Revisit those points after the first term. That review turns procurement into an accountable operational decision instead of a one-time presentation followed by an assumption that adoption will happen on its own.

Choose using acceptance evidence

Use these checks when reviewing the process with the people who will operate it. Record what was demonstrated and assign any unresolved issue to an owner before expanding the rollout.
