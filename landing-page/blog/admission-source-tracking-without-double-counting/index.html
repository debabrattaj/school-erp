Admission Source Tracking Without Counting the Same Family Twice

Separate enquiry sources, returning visits and sibling applications when reviewing admission campaign results.

Introduction

A parent might discover a school through a recommendation, fill in a website form and later call reception. Counting these as three new families inflates demand and makes campaign comparisons unreliable. Keep the enquiry identity separate from the contacts and channels through which the conversation develops.

Define the unit before making the report

Choose whether the report counts families, prospective students or applications. A sibling enquiry can represent one family and two potential admissions. Record an original discovery source and later contact channels in different fields. Do not overwrite the original source every time someone calls from another channel.

Potential duplicates need human review. A shared phone number may belong to siblings, a guardian or an admission coordinator; it is a signal to investigate, not proof that two student enquiries are identical. Preserve the contact history when records are linked or consolidated.

Worked example

One parent submits enquiries for Classes 2 and 5, then books a campus visit by phone. A family-level report counts one family, an applicant-level report counts two prospective students, and a contact report includes the phone conversation. All three numbers can be correct when their units are stated. None should be labelled simply “leads” without a definition.

Keep comparisons consistent

Use the same reporting window and unit when comparing sources. Show incomplete source data rather than assigning it to the largest campaign. Review a sample of records before relying on conversion percentages. A small source with several qualified applications may deserve attention even if its raw contact volume is low.

Separate a person, an applicant and an interaction

A school can count several legitimate things during admissions. A family is a relationship group, a prospective student is an individual applicant and an interaction is a call, message or visit. The same conversation may concern two prospective students. A reliable report keeps these units distinguishable before calculating totals.

Write the unit beside each measure. “New student enquiries” should mean something different from “contact attempts completed”. Avoid a generic leads total that changes meaning between the reception register and the leadership dashboard. The definitions should be understandable to the staff collecting the information, not just the person preparing a chart.

When reviewing a number, ask which event adds one to it. If a second phone call adds another new enquiry despite being connected to the same applicant, the report measures contact volume rather than unique demand. That may still be useful, but it must be labelled accurately.

Keep original discovery separate from later contact channels

A family's discovery source answers how it first learned about the school, to the extent that the family knows and chooses to tell the office. A contact channel answers how the current interaction occurred. A recommendation followed by a website form and a phone call is not necessarily three separate discovery sources.

Use distinct fields or an equivalent approved record structure. Preserve the reported discovery source when later interactions occur. Record a change only when the original entry was corrected or the reporting definition explicitly allows another treatment, and keep enough context to explain that change.

Do not force a precise answer when the parent is unsure. “Not known” is more honest than assigning the enquiry to whichever campaign was running that week. The uncertainty is useful information about the limits of the report and should not be hidden to make a source chart look complete.

Define a source list that staff can apply

Choose categories that reflect the questions the school wants to answer. A broad category such as recommendation may be sufficient for one review, while another may need a distinction between an existing family and a community event. Avoid collecting detailed attribution that nobody uses or that staff cannot explain consistently.

Keep an approved meaning for each category. “Website” could refer to discovery through search, a direct visit after a recommendation or merely the place where an application was submitted. Decide which meaning applies before collecting the data, and keep submission channels in a separate field if needed.

Review unusual entries without silently rewriting them into a convenient category. If a new source is genuinely useful, add it through the reporting owner's process and explain how it will be treated in comparisons. Frequent ad hoc category changes make a trend difficult to interpret.

Create a review queue for suspected duplicates

Potential duplicates should be reviewed, not automatically discarded because two fields match. A shared phone number can legitimately belong to siblings or several applications managed by one authorised contact. Similar names can refer to different people, while the same family can use more than one contact detail.

Give reviewers the context required to decide whether records should be linked, kept separate or handled through an approved consolidation process. Preserve the application reference and relevant interaction history. The review outcome should explain the relationship without losing evidence of what was originally received.

Track unresolved cases separately in the report. If the school cannot yet determine whether two records represent one applicant, do not present the unique count as perfectly settled. A provisional count with an explained review queue is more dependable than an apparently precise total based on unrecorded guesses.

Handle siblings without erasing separate applications

A sibling enquiry illustrates why family and applicant counts need different definitions. One parent may ask about two classes in one conversation. The family relationship should be preserved, but each prospective student may require a separate application, placement decision and eventual outcome.

If one sibling enrols and the other does not, a family-level report and an applicant-level report will describe different results. Decide which question each report is intended to answer. A school evaluating workload may need applicant counts; a communication review may need distinct families contacted.

Do not copy a sibling's discovery answer without making the relationship clear where the reporting process requires it. The office may reasonably record a shared family source, but that should be an understood rule. It should not arise accidentally because staff duplicate a row and forget which fields describe the individual applicant.

Set reporting periods around events, not convenient exports

Specify the date used to include a record in a source report. The first enquiry date, application date and enrolment date refer to different stages. A file downloaded this month can contain enquiries first received earlier, so the export date is not itself a useful admission cohort definition.

Keep activity and cohort views separate. A monthly activity view might show enquiries received and visits completed during that month. A source cohort view might follow applicants first recorded during a particular period and later show their outcomes. Neither should borrow the other's denominator without explanation.

When comparisons cross admission seasons, confirm that definitions remained consistent. A new duplicate-review process can reduce counted enquiries even if family interest did not change. Record such methodological changes so leadership does not attribute every movement in a chart to a marketing campaign.

Use a worked example with three different totals

Imagine a fictional week with six families contacting the school. Two families enquire for two children each, so there are eight prospective students. Across forms, calls and visits, the office records thirteen interactions. The valid totals are six families, eight prospective students and thirteen interactions, provided those relationships are confirmed.

Suppose a repeated website form initially appears as a ninth prospective student. The duplicate review links it to an existing applicant and preserves the additional interaction. The corrected applicant count returns to eight; the contact history remains useful rather than being deleted to make the report neat.

If four of the eight prospective students later enrol, the applicant-cohort conversion is four divided by eight, or fifty percent. A family conversion measure needs its own rule and relationship check. It cannot be inferred from the applicant percentage because the two siblings in a family may have different outcomes.

Do not let attribution become a claim about causation

A reported source tells the school something about how families describe discovery. It does not prove that one campaign alone caused an admission. Families can encounter several messages and recommendations before deciding to enquire, and they may remember some of those contacts more clearly than others.

Use source data as a prompt for investigation rather than as a complete explanation of demand. A small source with several completed applications may merit attention, but its count can be unstable. A large source may produce early-stage questions that take longer to progress. Compare outcomes with their context and reporting period.

Avoid unsupported statements about guaranteed return or campaign superiority. If the school combines source data with costs, the responsible decision-makers should define the comparison and its limitations. The admissions register should supply reliable observations, not manufacture certainty that the recorded information cannot support.

Review unknown and corrected sources openly

Unknown source values are not merely a data-cleaning inconvenience. They show where the office lacks an answer or where the question was not asked. Keep them visible so the report does not exaggerate the share of known categories.

When correcting a source entry, record the basis of the correction. A parent clarifying an earlier answer is different from a staff member deciding that all website forms must belong to an online campaign. The first adds evidence; the second changes the classification rule and should be treated accordingly.

Review the collection process if unknown values are common. The question may be unclear, asked at an inconvenient point or irrelevant to the immediate conversation. A short, respectful question with an option not to answer can provide more useful information than an intrusive demand for precision at the start of an enquiry.

Connect source records to outcomes without duplicating history

A source report becomes more useful when an enquiry can be linked to the relevant application and eventual outcome. Use stable references and the school's supported record relationships rather than matching records by name after the season ends.

Preserve the original enquiry context when an application progresses. Creating a fresh disconnected record at each stage makes the source disappear or encourages staff to enter another source value from memory. A linked history lets the report follow the same applicant without counting every transition as a new opportunity.

Where historical records cannot be linked confidently, record that limitation. Do not fill missing relationships solely to improve a conversion chart. An incomplete but honest connection is preferable to attributing an enrolment to the wrong family or campaign. Review a sample of linked records before relying on the aggregated result.

Prepare a source report that explains its own limits

Include the reporting period, counted unit, source definition and treatment of duplicates in the report note. Show the relevant counts alongside percentages. A reader should be able to understand whether the chart describes families, applicants or interactions without asking the analyst.

Identify provisional records and unresolved source values. If the report excludes them, state the exclusion and its effect on the denominator. A percentage of known sources is not the same as a percentage of all enquiries, even when the chart labels look similar.

Keep the purpose of the report visible. Reception may use a channel view to plan response capacity, while leadership uses an applicant cohort view to understand progression. Sharing the same unlabeled chart with both groups can invite contradictory interpretations. A concise definition note prevents that confusion without overwhelming the report with technical detail.

Share attribution at the level the review needs

A leadership discussion about discovery sources usually needs grouped counts and definitions, not a list of every parent's contact details. Prepare the report for its audience and keep record-level investigation within the authorised admissions workflow. If a particular classification needs review, assign that case to the appropriate staff rather than circulating the full enquiry export.

Keep the reporting date on shared copies. A duplicate correction or newly recorded outcome can change the figures after an export, so an old attachment should not silently become the official season result. Identify which version supersedes earlier copies when a report is revised.

Use small-group results carefully in discussion. Even without names, detailed combinations can reveal which family is being described to people familiar with the admissions activity. Choose the level of detail needed for the decision and avoid adding private context simply to make a campaign anecdote more memorable.

Test the collection process before the next campaign

Use fictional cases to rehearse the records: one parent making two calls, two siblings in one form, a family with an unknown discovery source and a returning applicant from an earlier year. Ask staff to show which records they would create or link and which counters should change.

Compare the answers across reception, counsellors and the reporting owner. Disagreement indicates a definition or workflow problem to resolve before live collection expands. Write the agreed treatment into a short operating note and keep the examples available for new staff.

In a Schoolment demonstration, ask how the configured admission records support these relationships and what reporting steps remain manual. Verify the actual behaviour instead of assuming that any chart labelled leads uses the school's intended unit. Reliable source tracking begins with clear identities and consistent events, not with a more colourful dashboard.
