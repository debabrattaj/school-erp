School ERP vs LMS: Which Does Your School Actually Need?

A function-by-function comparison of a school ERP and a learning management system — who uses each, where they overlap, what it costs to get the choice wrong, and how to run both if you must.

Published September 2, 2026

The distinction in one line each

A school ERP runs the school: admissions, fees and accounts, attendance, timetable, exams and report cards, staff, campus operations, and communication with parents. Its users are the office, management and parents.

An LMS runs the lessons: course content, delivery, assignments and submissions, quizzes, and each student's progress through learning material. Its users are teachers and students.

Both are sold as "school software", which is where the confusion starts and where a meaningful amount of money gets spent on the wrong thing.

Which system answers which question

The fastest way to place any requirement is to ask which question it resembles:

  • "Is this student's fee paid, and when did the last payment clear?" — ERP.
  • "Did this student complete the assigned reading for unit 3?" — LMS.
  • "What is this student's attendance this term, and what goes on the report card?" — ERP.
  • "Which topic did the class score worst on in the practice quiz?" — LMS.
  • "Which enquiries from last month have not been followed up?" — ERP.
  • "Has this student submitted, and was it late?" — LMS.
  • "Which bus is this child on, and where is it?" — ERP.
  • "Can the class see the recorded lesson from Tuesday?" — LMS.

Function by function

Where each system is the authority, and where neither clearly is:

  • Admissions — ERP only. Enquiry pipeline, online application form, admission tests, conversion to enrolled student.
  • Fees and accounts — ERP only. Fee structures, billing, online collection, receipts, concessions, ledger, payroll.
  • School-day attendance and timetable — ERP only. An LMS may mark presence in an online class; it does not run period-wise attendance with a timetable behind it.
  • Formal exams and report cards — ERP. Weighted grading, rank, board-shaped cards, multi-year academic history.
  • Course content and delivery — LMS only. Video, sequencing, packaged courseware.
  • Assignments and submissions — LMS, though homework as a parent-visible list sits in the ERP.
  • Quizzes and auto-grading — the genuine overlap. Both do it, for different purposes.
  • Campus operations, staff records, documents — ERP only.
  • Parent communication — ERP. An LMS talks to students about coursework, not to families about fees.

The overlap, examined honestly

Three areas sit between the two, and this is where duplicate spending happens.

Assessment. An LMS quiz measures progress through material the LMS delivered. A school exam is an administrative record the school is accountable for, weighted across test types and ending in a report card. Schoolment includes auto-graded online tests as a licensed add-on precisely so a school does not need a second platform for the second case.

Homework. An LMS treats it as a submission workflow; an ERP treats it as something parents can see. Which you need depends on whether the work is submitted digitally or in a book.

Syllabus and lesson plans. Held per subject in Schoolment, so coverage through the term is a record. An LMS holds the material itself; the ERP holds the plan.

What it costs to get the choice wrong

The two failure modes are not symmetrical, which is why the sequencing advice below is not neutral.

Buying an LMS when you needed an ERP leaves the expensive problems untouched. Fees still reconcile by hand, admissions enquiries still go cold, the attendance register still cannot be produced for the board, and report card season still consumes three weeks. Teachers may be happier; the office is not, and the money is still leaking.

Buying an ERP when you needed an LMS is the cheaper mistake. Administration improves, and the teaching-side gap shows up as teachers using free tools alongside — which is untidy but rarely damaging. Notably, some platforms marketed as school management systems are LMS-heavy and administratively thin, and at least one well-known vendor discontinued its fee module while still appearing on school-ERP shortlists. Verify module availability directly before assuming coverage.

Which to buy first, and why

For most K-12 schools in India, the ERP comes first. Not because teaching matters less, but because administrative failures cost money and compliance standing while a missing content platform costs convenience.

A useful test: list the ten tasks consuming the most staff time this month. If eight are administrative — chasing fees, compiling marks, answering parent calls, following up enquiries — buy the ERP and revisit the LMS question next year. If most are about delivering and marking digital coursework, you have a genuine LMS requirement and should treat it as core infrastructure.

If you run both, decide the authority first

This is the whole integration question, and it has one right answer. The ERP owns student identity and enrolment, because it owns admission, class placement, transfers and withdrawals. The LMS consumes that list rather than maintaining its own.

Get this wrong and two student lists diverge within a term: a student admitted in August who never appears in the LMS, a withdrawn student who still has course access in December, and a reconciliation job that lands on the person you bought software to unburden.

  • Sync direction: ERP to LMS, one way, for students, sections and staff.
  • Decide where the mark of record lives — if an LMS quiz counts towards a report card, the ERP must hold the final figure.
  • Decide which system parents are told to use, and do not give them two logins for overlapping information.
  • Agree who removes access when a student leaves, and when.
  • Bulk spreadsheet import and CSV exports make this practical without a custom integration project.

Questions to ask each vendor

The same five questions, asked of both, surface most of what matters:

  • What do you deliberately not do? A confident answer means someone made scoping decisions on purpose. Ours: Schoolment is an ERP, not a content-delivery LMS — no video hosting, no SCORM, no self-paced learning paths — though homework, syllabus and lesson plans, and auto-graded online tests are included.
  • Who is the authority for student records in your model?
  • Which modules are licensed separately? Online tests, exam proctoring and biometric attendance are our add-ons; the rest of the suite is switched on per school.
  • Show the task, do not describe it — a report card generated from marks, or a course assigned to a section, live.
  • Can we export all of our own data if we leave?

FAQ

Common questions

What is the main difference between a school ERP and an LMS?

A school ERP is the system of record for running the school — admissions, fees and accounts, attendance, timetable, exams and report cards, staff, campus operations and parent communication. An LMS is the system for running lessons — course content, delivery, assignments, quizzes and progress through learning material. Different users, different questions: the ERP answers "is this fee paid and what is this student's attendance", the LMS answers "has this student completed unit 3".

Can one system do both?

Partly, and the honest answer is that nobody does both deeply. Most ERPs include some teaching-side functions — homework, syllabus, online tests — and most LMS platforms have added light administrative features. Schools with substantial self-paced digital coursework generally end up running both. Schools whose teaching happens in a classroom usually find the ERP's academic modules sufficient.

Which should a school buy first?

The ERP, for most K-12 schools. Administrative failures — fee leakage, a missed admissions season, an attendance record you cannot produce for the board — cost money and standing, while a missing content platform costs convenience. The exception is a school whose teaching model depends on digital delivery, where the LMS is core infrastructure and the ERP question follows it.

If we run both, how do they stay in sync?

Make the ERP the authority for student identity and enrolment, and have the LMS consume that list one way rather than maintaining its own. It owns admission, class placement, transfers and withdrawals, so it is the only system that knows the truth about who is enrolled. Two independently maintained lists diverge within a term, and reconciling them lands on the staff member you bought software to unburden.

Does Schoolment include LMS features?

Some, deliberately scoped. Homework set against a class and section and visible to parents, syllabus and lesson plans held per subject, a multi-curriculum planner, and auto-graded online tests and exam proctoring as licensed add-ons. Not included: video hosting, SCORM packages, self-paced learning paths and course discussion forums. If content delivery is central to how your school teaches, you need a real LMS alongside.
